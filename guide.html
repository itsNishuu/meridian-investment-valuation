What is Meridian?

Meridian works out what a company is worth. It values three fictional Indian mid-sized companies in several different ways, shows where the methods agree and disagree, and points out which assumptions the answer depends on most.

Who uses tools like this
Investment banking, equity research, corporate finance and FP&A analysts
The data
Three fictional companies with invented peers and deals

The problem it solves

If you were buying a local business, you would ask two things: how much profit will it make in future, and what have similar businesses sold for? Valuing a company is the same idea on a larger scale.

Analysts use several methods because each one has blind spots, and a single "the share is worth ₹X" hides how fragile that number is. A small change in one assumption can move it a lot. Meridian puts every method on one chart and shows exactly how sensitive the answer is.

The story inside the app

Three fictional companies: Helios Precision Systems (industrial components), Saffron Food Packaging (consumer packaging) and Nexa Mobility Components (electric-vehicle supply chain). Each has a five-year forecast, a set of invented similar companies and past takeover deals. Pick a company at the top of the page, and switch between Bear, Base and Bull views of the future.

A guided tour, one tab at a time

Each step below matches a tab along the top of the app. Read the step, open the tab, then try the suggested change.

    SummaryWhat is it worth, by each method?

    The “football field” chart: each bar is the range of value per share that one method supports. Where the bars overlap, the methods agree. Below it: the walk from the value of the whole business to the value of one share, and review points the model raises by itself.

    Try this: With Helios selected, read the first review point: one method implies a much lower price-to-profit multiple than similar companies trade at.

    Open Summary →
  2. 2

    ModelWhere does the number come from?

    The forecast: sales, profit, investment and cash for five years, each converted into today's money. Blue numbers are assumptions you can change; black numbers are calculated, the same convention banks use.

    Try this: Raise WACC (the return investors require) by one point and watch the value per share fall.

    SensitivityHow sure is the answer?

    Tables showing the value per share as two key assumptions change together, and a “tornado” chart ranking which single assumption moves the answer most.

    Try this: Check that the outlined cell equals the base-case price, then see which assumption sits at the top of the tornado.

Key words, in everyday language

Valuation
An estimate of what a company or one of its shares is worth.
DCF (discounted cash flow)
Valuing a company by forecasting the cash it will generate and converting future cash into today's money.
Free cash flow
Cash the business generates after paying its running costs, taxes and investment.
WACC (discount rate)
The yearly return investors and lenders expect. A higher rate makes future cash worth less today.
Terminal value
The value of all the cash beyond the five-year forecast, usually a large part of the total.
Enterprise vs equity value
Enterprise value is the whole business. Equity value is what is left for shareholders after debt is repaid and cash is added.
EBITDA
Operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.
Multiple (EV/EBITDA)
Value divided by profit, a quick way to compare companies of different sizes.
Comparables
Similar listed companies used as a reference for value.
Precedent transactions
Past takeovers of similar companies, and the prices paid.
Football field
A chart of value ranges from each method, side by side.
Sensitivity analysis
Testing how much the answer changes when an assumption changes.
Bear / Base / Bull
Pessimistic, central and optimistic views of the future.

What this project shows, and what it does not

What it shows

Forecast-driven valuation, the links between the three financial statements, sensitivity analysis and the presentation conventions of banking work. Relevant to valuation, financial modelling, FP&A and investment-banking analyst roles.

What it is not

It is not a recommendation to buy or sell anything. The companies, peers and deals are fictional, and no real market data is used.
